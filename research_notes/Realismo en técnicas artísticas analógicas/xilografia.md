## 1. Carving: tools, marks, tone, following form, chatter, cut edges, plank vs end grain

### Takeaway
A relief print is a binary stamp of the uncut surface: everything cut away is paper colour, everything left standing prints flat ink. All tone is therefore made by the *geometry of cuts*: V-gouge lines that taper to points at both ends (width = depth of cut), U-gouge strokes with rounded ends and even width, knife-cut black lines (black-line) or gouged white lines (white-line), plus accidental "chatter" where cleared areas are too shallow. Plank (side-grain) woodcut gives coarser, bolder marks than end-grain wood engraving, which alone allows very fine white lines and dense cross-hatching.

### Cited Findings
**Tool marks (what each gouge leaves in the print)**
- V-gouge lines vary in width and end in pointed/tapered tips because the tool dips in and lifts out; fine V-tools are used for cross-hatching to build tone/texture. — [Handprinted, "Using different tools to make marks on lino"](https://handprinted.co.uk/blogs/blog/using-different-tools-to-make-marks-on-lino); [older copy](https://blog.handprinted.co.uk/?p=70)
- U-gouge lines are of even width with rounded ends; U-gouges leave flatter floors and are used to clear large areas. — [Handprinted](https://handprinted.co.uk/blogs/blog/using-different-tools-to-make-marks-on-lino)
- Rocking a U-gouge while pushing gives a wavy line; tapping it straight down gives a dotted (stipple) texture; "snapping off" a V-cut gives a clean stop. — [Handprinted](https://handprinted.co.uk/blogs/blog/using-different-tools-to-make-marks-on-lino)
- Marks differ by material: vinyl/soft-cut lino behaves differently from traditional hessian-backed lino. — [Handprinted](https://handprinted.co.uk/blogs/blog/using-different-tools-to-make-marks-on-lino)
- In woodcutting, if the tool's nose slips under the surface the cut becomes ragged, and a line close to a printing edge can break off. — [woodblock.com encyclopedia, "Woodcutting"](https://woodblock.com/encyclopedia/entries/011_04/woodcutting.html)
- On a plank block a single white line needs four knife cuts (two for the sides of the trench, one for each end); the burin/graver suits hard end-grain but "tears up the fibre and destroys the line" on plank. — [Dodgson 1903, catalogue of early woodcuts (Heidelberg digi)](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/dodgson1903bd1/0042); [next page](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/dodgson1903bd1/0043)
- A knife can still be used on end grain. — [Dodgson 1903](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/dodgson1903bd1/0043)

**Black-line vs white-line**
- Black-line: the cutter removes the background, leaving the drawn lines raised (lines print black). White-line: the lines/shapes themselves are cut away, so the background prints and the image reads as white on a mostly black field; white lines were cut with different tools than the knife/chisel used to clear background in black-line work. Early examples (German, c. 1500) and Urs Graf are credited with the white-line method. — [Fitzwilliam Museum, Anonymous German School](https://fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/dept/pdp/prints/highlights/20.html)
- 19th-c. source: white line is "the peculiar province of the wood-engraver"; cutting fine white lines is far less difficult than cutting fine black lines. — [A History of Wood-Engraving (wikisource copy)](https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/Page%3AA_History_of_Wood-Engraving.djvu/186)
- Attribution conflict: Graf is the usual attribution; some blogs credit Bewick with the white-line method (Bewick popularised it in end-grain engraving). — [Fitzwilliam](https://fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/dept/pdp/prints/highlights/20.html); contradicted by [blog](https://mathewsfusent.blogspot.com/2022/11/creating-woodcuts-enabled-artists-to.html)

**Plank (side grain) vs end grain**
- Wood engraving = hard end-grain (usually boxwood) cut across the grain with engraving tools; woodcut = softer side-grain plank cut along the grain with knife and gouges. A finely engraved white line indicates wood engraving rather than woodcut. Burin enables intricate cross-hatching and tonal variation. — [Wood engraving (wikiwand)](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Wood-engraver); [Dodgson 1903](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/dodgson1903bd1/0042)
- Bewick engraved harder woods than woodcutters used and cut the end of the block instead of the side. — [Wood engraving (wikiwand)](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Wood-engraver)
- End-grain blocks stand up to impression better over long runs. — [Briar Press forum, "endgrain or plankside?"](https://briarpress.org/32918)
- The soft side grain used for most woodcuts "is less suitable for fine detail". — reported in the Brücke search results (attribution ambiguous between [Henze & Ketterer, Expressive woodcuts Brücke style](https://henze-ketterer.ch/ausstellungen/expressive-woodcuts-brucke-style) and [Sotheby's, 7 things about German Expressionist prints](https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/7-things-you-need-to-know-about-german-expressionist-prints))

**How tone and form are built (refined tradition)**
- Dürer combined dense hatching with lines that swell and shrink to give convincing shading and volume; a special cross-hatching technique extracted fine detail from the wood. — [eGuide Zürich, Apocalypse](https://eguide.ch/en/?p=1651); [eGuide, woodcut cycles](https://eguide.ch/en/?p=2839)
- Met: in *Saint Michael Fighting the Dragon* Dürer modulated light/dark and suggested depth "with lines that swell and taper". — [Met Museum 360117](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/360117)
- NGA: typical pre-Dürer woodcuts had heavy outlines around relatively flat forms with only occasional parallel hatching for shadow; Dürer's lines vary in length, spacing, curvature and thickness, modelling forms roundly. — [NGA, Apocalypse of Saint John](https://www.nga.gov/collection/art-object-page.141215.html)

**Chatter / residual wood in cleared areas**
- "Chatter" or "noise" = ink picked up by carved-away (insufficiently deep) areas, printing as faint lines/marks; some artists keep it as character, others mask it (Mylar mask cut to the uncarved design). — [Handprinted, Mylar mask to reduce chatter](https://handprinted.co.uk/blogs/blog/using-a-mylar-mask-to-reduce-chatter-in-a-linocut)
- Sellers describe chatter and embossing as visible evidence of the hand process; stray marks are sometimes left intentionally. — [Artfinder listing](https://www.artfinder.com/product/untitled-6386e/)

**Observations from NPR literature about real woodcuts**
- Mello, Jung & Walter (2007) was the first method to synthesise woodcuts from photos; per Mesquita & Walter (2019) quoting it, "in real woodcuts the brightest regions are white, while the darkest ones are black, in both cases without any discernible texture"; Mesquita & Walter also state that, from observation of real woodcuts, they draw the borders of segmented regions as black. — [Mesquita & Walter, "Reaction-diffusion Woodcuts", VISIGRAPP 2019 (PDF)](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2019/73859/73859.pdf); [repository](https://lume.ufrgs.br/handle/10183/194383)

### Inferences
- Rendering rule (V-gouge stroke): model each cut as a lens-shaped white mark whose width ∝ depth profile (0 → max → 0), so both ends are pointed; vary length and maximum width with tone (darker tone = thinner/shorter white cuts; lighter tone = wider cuts that eventually merge into cleared white).
- Rendering rule (U-gouge stroke): constant width, round caps on both ends, slightly scalloped side edges; used for clearing large white areas in overlapping parallel passes, which leaves small residual ridges ("chatter") only where passes don't overlap.
- Rendering rule (stab/stipple): U-gouge taps → small crescent/round white dots, used for mid-tone texture in expressive linocut.
- Tone mapping should be essentially 3-zone, consistent with Mello's observation: pure black (no cuts) for darkest, pure paper white (fully cleared, possibly with chatter) for brightest, and line-pattern modulation only in mid-tones. Avoid noise/texture inside full-white and full-black zones except for physically motivated effects (chatter in white, grain/speckle in black).
- Black-line vs white-line should be two distinct modes: white-line = black field with white cuts that follow form (Vallotton, Graf, TGP), black-line = white field with standing black contour lines and hatching (Dürer, early book woodcuts).
- Plank woodcut cannot do fine white cross-hatching; a "realistic woodcut" variant should avoid crossing white lines (which read as wood engraving) and keep minimum white-line width relatively large; cross-hatching belongs to a "wood engraving" or "Dürer black-line" look (where crossing *black* lines are left standing by cutting out the diamond-shaped interstices).
- Ragged/broken edges: thin standing black ridges near cuts should occasionally break or chip (gap in a black line), especially across grain.

### Gaps
- Could not read the full Handprinted, woodblock.com or Barenforum pages (blocked); no measured data on cut widths, ridge heights or chip frequency.

## 2. Wood grain in prints: when it shows and what it looks like

### Takeaway
Grain prints when the block surface is not perfectly flat: soft woods with strong earlywood/latewood contrast, brushed or weathered planks, or deliberately uncut "grain blocks". It appears as long, roughly parallel, slightly wavy streaks of lighter ink (soft earlywood sinks and prints less) inside solid areas, often with knots; in colour woodcuts it is frequently printed as its own layer (Munch) or used pictorially for water and sky (Nolde).

### Cited Findings
- Munch deliberately used the grain of the wood as a design feature. — [Wallraf-Richartz-Museum](https://www.wallraf.museum/index.php?L=1&id=293)
- In *The Kiss IV* the couple comes from one carved block, while a seemingly unworked background block with a vertical grain pattern supplies the rest. — [Nasjonalmuseet, The Kiss IV](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.K_H.A.19517)
- In *Moonlight* Munch printed an additional block with visible woodgrain over the image. — [V&A, Moonlight (Munch)](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1108276/moonlight-woodcut-munch-edvard/)
- MoMA: with each cut Munch "met the resistance of the grain". — [MoMA, Evening. Melancholy I](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/73795)
- Munch's "jigsaw" method: block sawn into pieces (fretsaw), each piece inked separately in its own colour and reassembled for one printing; the saw kerfs print as thin white lines between colour areas. — [MoMA, Evening. Melancholy I](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/73795); [Sparebankstiftelsen, Towards the Forest I](https://sparebankstiftelsen.no/en/artworks/towards-the-forest-i/)
- Nolde often incorporated knots, grain and imperfections of the wood; in *Prophet* (1912) coarsely gouged areas, jagged lines and textured grain combine. — [MoMA, Prophet](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/62151); [MFAH, Prophet](https://emuseum.mfah.org/objects/24215/prophet)
- Nolde used grain pictorially: in *Fishing Steamer* (1910) for the swell of the sea; in *Despair* (1906) the grain is the basis for waves and clouds. — [UMMA, Verzweiflung (Despair)](https://umma.umich.edu/objects/verzweiflung-despair-1965-2-72)
- Wire brushes remove soft earlywood faster than hard latewood, raising the grain as relief; contrast depends on species (strong in Southern yellow pine, weaker in Eastern white pine). Brushing across the grain creates ragged fibres; plywood face veneers give little grain depth; wetting alone starts to raise grain. — [Fine Woodworking forum, texturizing wood grain](https://www.finewoodworking.com/forum/texturizing-wood-grain)
- Printmakers use soft copper-wire brushing on pine planks to expose grain (not recommended for water-based mokuhanga). — [Barenforum encyclopedia, "Carving techniques for creating texture" (James Mundie)](https://barenforum.org/encyclopedia/entries/015_01/015_01.html)
- Scorch-and-brush (yakisugi) blocks: char is rubbed off along the grain with a wire brush; the brush itself leaves incidental small scratch lines, and some char remains. — [Invisible Sun design blog, Yakisugi wood grain printmaking](https://invisiblesundesign.blogspot.com/2020/08/yakisugi-wood-grain-printmaking-pt-1.html)
- Printing directly from wood grain is a recognised relief technique in its own right. — [The Curious Printmaker, Print from wood grain](https://thecuriousprintmaker.co.uk/print-wood-grain/); [Handprinted, Relief printing from wood grain](https://handprinted.co.uk/blogs/blog/relief-printing-from-wood-grain)
- In mokuhanga, effects "like woodgrain textures" depend on humidity, paper type and baren choice. — [Beaty Museum (UBC), Mokuhanga process](https://explore.beatymuseum.ubc.ca/peatland/process/mokuhanga/)
- Mizuno et al. treat grain, ink colour and moisture as the factors that change the finish of a virtual woodblock print. — [Mizuno et al., CGF 2000, multicolor virtual woodblock printing](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8659.00397)

### Inferences
- Rendering rule: grain is a *multiplicative attenuation of ink inside printing areas*, never ink in cleared areas. Model as a 1D-dominant noise stretched ~20–80× along a single grain direction per block, with gentle waviness and occasional knots (elliptical whorls where streaks bend around). Earlywood bands = slightly lighter ink (partial transfer), latewood = full ink.
- Strength depends on variant: realistic/Japanese (cherry, shina, fine paper, baren) = very faint grain mostly visible in large flat colour areas; expressive (pine/plank, wire-brushed) = strong streaks also in blacks; a "grain block" layer can be printed in a light colour under/over the key (Munch).
- All layers cut from the same plank share the grain direction; different blocks in a multi-block print may have different grain orientation and pattern (useful cue for realism in 2-3 colour modes).
- Grain streaks should be visible even in "solid black": slightly lighter, lower-density streaks (dark grey/brown-black) rather than white, unless the ink film is thin.
- Cuts across grain → rougher, more chipped edges; cuts along grain → cleaner but may "run" (splinter extending along grain). (Inference, unsourced.)
- Jigsaw mode: colour areas separated by a constant-width thin white gap (saw kerf, ~1 mm at print scale) is a strong Munch-like cue for a 2/3-colour variant.

### Gaps
- No quantitative grain spacing (rings/cm) or contrast measurements found.
- No conservation study of grain visibility vs ink film thickness found.

## 3. Inking and printing: roller, ink film, pressure, speckle, starvation, halo, embossing, show-through

### Takeaway
Real relief impressions are rarely uniform: a thin roller-applied ink film plus uneven hand pressure gives mottled, "salty" solids where the paper's high points take ink and the low points stay white; too much ink or pressure gives a darker rim/halo and filled-in fine lines; pressure embosses the paper so the image is visible/feelable from the back. Hand burnishing (baren/spoon) is softer and more variable than a press.

### Cited Findings
- Correct ink on the slab has a "sandpaper-like" texture; if the ink on the brayer is too thick it fills in the detailed lines of the plate. — [Galveston Arts Center printmaking lesson (PDF)](https://www.galvestonartscenter.org/s/Printmaking-Lesson-1-Art-Club-enp8.pdf)
- Ink should be rolled gently in different directions to avoid under- or over-inking; change one variable at a time (ink amount, pressure, paper). — [Art Noise, relief printing troubleshooting](https://shop.artnoise.ca/blogs/art-noise-handbook/relief-printing-with-lino-blocks-step-by-step-plus-troubleshooting-for-cleaner-prints)
- Too-textured paper gives broken lines; oil-based ink generally gives more even, crisp coverage. — [Artbeatbox, linocut guide](https://www.artbeatbox.com/blog/the-complete-guide-to-linocut-printmaking)
- Speckled under-inked pulls are attributed to ink drying too fast, ink being absorbed by the block, or paper too absorbent; printers re-ink and re-lay the paper to fix patches. — [r/printmaking thread (mirror)](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/printmaking/comments/1f8rqvl/what_am_i_doing_wrong)
- With a baren (lower pressure than a press) and stiff oil-based ink, "the ink coverage will not be as even or solid, but many people appreciate the softer, mottled effect achieved with a baren". — attributed by the search tool to supplier pages ([McClain's baren catalog](https://www.imcclains.com/catalog/baren/index.html) / [Japanese Paper Place, Baren](https://www.japanesepaperplace.com/news/baren-making-prints-without-a-press)); exact page not verified
- Relief pressure leaves an embossed pattern on the other side of the sheet that can be seen or felt. — [HKU UMAG, Relief print](https://ulearninglabs.umag.hku.hk/en/?p=3434)
- A press is usually needed to get deep embossing with dampened rag paper; with a baren one is effectively making a dry-paper print. (Context: collagraph/intaglio-relief discussion.) — [WetCanvas, collagraph sans press](https://www.wetcanvas.com/forums/topic/collagraph-sans-press)
- Halo (letterpress/flexo): excess ink accumulating at the edges of printed letters/dots and/or a coloured zone around ink piles from the spreading ink vehicle. — [PrintWiki, Halo Effect](https://printwiki.org/Halo_Effect)
- Causes of edge halo: excessive impression pressure forcing ink beyond the image edge; uneven pressure makes halos vary across the sheet; very fluid ink. — [Luminite, flexo halo defects](https://blog.luminite.com/blog/halo-flexo-printing-defects); [Flexopedia](https://flexopedia.net/?p=1777)
- Letterpress: over-dampened sheets can let the oil of the ink spread and produce a halo/discoloration around letters. — [Briar Press, discoloration around edges of letters](https://briarpress.org/31838)
- Gauguin proofed his Noa Noa blocks by hand rather than on a press, varying pressure and inking from impression to impression; no two impressions are alike; he sometimes printed slightly off-register for a blurred effect, and printed one block in yellow then overprinted in black. — [Grob Gallery, Gauguin Noa Noa](https://grobgallery.com/artworks/1441)
- Gauguin scratched blocks with needle and sandpaper (borrowed from lithography) to obtain fine textures, combined with cutting and gouging. — [NGA Australia, Gauguin's World audio stop](https://nga.gov.au/audio-learning-tours/gauguins-world/stop/302)
- Nolde made texture on dancers' robes by pressing the block to the paper *without ink* (blind relief), and a second, inked impression compounded the texture. — [Cleveland Museum of Art, Two Dancers](https://www.clevelandart.org/articles/two-dancers); [FAMSF, Tänzerinnen](https://www.famsf.org/artworks/tanzerinnen-dancers)
- Mokuhanga: the baren forces pigment deep into the paper fibres; pressure is built gradually with circular strokes. — [Beaty Museum (UBC), Mokuhanga](https://explore.beatymuseum.ubc.ca/peatland/process/mokuhanga/)
- Mizuno et al. (Eurographics 2001) model the printing process physically, focusing on ink properties/behaviour and the effects of ink moisture; their virtual pipeline = carve block → spread ink → lay paper → rub with baren. — [Eurographics DL, "Improvement of the printing model in multi-colored and multi-woodblock virtual printing"](https://diglib.eg.org/items/79a2119a-86bd-4b47-bcd4-090c256c647f); [CGF 2000](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8659.00397)

### Inferences
- Rendering rule (speckle / "salty" solids): ink coverage in a solid = f(paper height map, local pressure, ink film). Pixels where paper height < threshold(pressure, ink) stay paper-coloured → small white pinholes clustered on paper texture, more frequent toward the centre of large solids under a baren (pressure falls off), and more frequent with thin ink. Do *not* use uniform random noise; use the paper's fibre/tooth texture as the threshold field so speckle aligns with paper structure.
- Rendering rule (baren mottle): low-frequency pressure field (overlapping circular/zig-zag strokes of a ~10–13 cm disc at print scale) modulating density by roughly ±10–25 % in solids; a press print instead is near-uniform with a slight gradient along the roller direction.
- Ink starvation: the second and later areas rolled with one charge of the brayer get less ink; large solids show lighter bands parallel to the roller travel, with width ≈ brayer width (inference, no direct source).
- Halo/squash: at the boundaries of black areas, add a 1–3 px darker rim (ink pushed to edges) and, with heavy pressure/fluid ink, a very faint, slightly lighter "oil" fringe outside the edge; fine white cuts narrower than a threshold should fill in (closing operation) when the ink film is thick.
- Embossing: generate a height map from the block (printing areas pressed into the paper) and apply subtle directional shading (emboss) to the paper texture, stronger in press/kimedashi modes; edges of cut-outs get a slight raised lip.
- Show-through (thin washi): optional effect only for "verso" views; low priority for photos rendered as the recto.
- Blind relief (Nolde, karazuri): an uninked block impression = embossing without ink, a strong realism cue for white areas.

### Gaps
- No source found giving ink film thickness in microns, typical brayer widths, or measured speckle density.
- "Salty" as a printmaking term was not found in sources; the term may be informal.
- No direct source for ink starvation banding from the brayer.
- Edge-halo sources are from flexo/letterpress, not woodcut specifically.

## 4. Paper: washi vs Western, damp vs dry, texture–ink interaction

### Takeaway
Japanese kōzo papers are thin, long-fibred, absorbent and strong when damp; printed damp with water-based pigment and a baren, the colour is absorbed into the fibres (soft, matte, slightly diffused) rather than sitting on top. Western printing papers used dry with oil inks keep the ink on the surface, with speckle following the paper's tooth. Paper moisture also affects registration.

### Cited Findings
- Traditional ukiyo-e paper is handmade kōzo (paper mulberry); it is absorbent, flexible and stable even when moistened; printers dampen paper so it takes the colour, and reduce excess moisture with blotting paper. — [MyLearning, Japanese woodblock materials, tools and techniques](https://www.mylearning.org/stories/japanese-woodblock-print/woodblock-materials-tools-and-techniques); [MAU glossary, blotting paper](https://art-design-glossary.musabi.ac.jp/?p=2427)
- Mokuhanga paper is dampened before printing; the damp sheet is aligned with the kento and covered with a backing sheet while rubbing. — [Jacqueline Gribbin, Water-based woodblock printmaking](https://jacquelinegribbin.squarespace.com/woodblock)
- Thin, strong Japanese fibres pull ink with minimal pressure (suited to hand printing); ~40–90 gsm Japanese papers are recommended for hand burnishing; heavier papers are harder to burnish evenly without a press. — [Hickman Design, best paper for lino printing](https://hickmandesign.co.uk/blog/equipment/what-is-the-best-paper-for-lino-printing) (vendor blog)
- Changes in moisture of blocks or paper (pigment saturation, ambient humidity) shift registration; printers corrected by recutting the kento. — [JAANUS, kentou](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/k/kentou.htm)
- Too textured a paper gives broken lines in relief printing. — [Artbeatbox](https://www.artbeatbox.com/blog/the-complete-guide-to-linocut-printmaking)
- Absorbent paper is one cause of speckled, under-inked prints. — [r/printmaking (mirror)](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/printmaking/comments/1f8rqvl/what_am_i_doing_wrong)
- TGP linocuts are typically printed in black on cream (not white) paper. — [Art Institute of Chicago, Firing Squad (Méndez)](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/160038/firing-squad)
- Mizuno et al. model paper/ink moisture as a primary variable of the virtual print's finish. — [CGF 2000](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8659.00397)

### Inferences
- Paper presets: (a) washi/kōzo: warm off-white, visible long fibres (thin, curvy, 5–20 mm at print scale, slightly lighter/translucent), low tooth; water-based colour slightly soft-edged and absorbed (multiply blend, gentle 0.5–1 px blur, density following fibres); (b) Western mould-made/cartridge (dry, oil ink): finer isotropic tooth, crisper edges, speckle in solids driven by tooth; (c) cheap cream/newsprint-like paper for TGP/poster looks.
- Ink should never be rendered as a perfectly flat fill over paper: multiply ink over the paper texture, then knock out speckle where paper valleys aren't reached.
- Damp printing → softer edges and more even solids; dry printing with baren → more speckle and mottle.

### Gaps
- No quantitative fibre-length/opacity data or measured paper height maps found; dosa sizing (glue + alum) of washi and its effect on bleeding was not covered by the results.
- Western paper names (Somerset, Stonehenge, Fabriano) and their surfaces were not covered by the sources found.

## 5. Colour woodcuts: key block + colour blocks, reduction, ukiyo-e/moku-hanga, bokashi, registration and overprinting

### Takeaway
Colour relief prints are stacks of flat (or gradated) layers, each from its own block (or from successive states of one block in reduction). Registration is mechanical (kento corner + edge marks) and drifts with paper moisture, so small offsets, slivers of paper between colours and overlaps are typical. Ukiyo-e pigments are transparent, so overprints mix (blue over yellow = green) and colour over the black key leaves it black; gradations (bokashi) are made by wiping/brushing the block, in recognisable shapes (horizontal bands at the top of skies, irregular cloud-like fades).

### Cited Findings
**Registration**
- Kento: two marks cut into each block — *kagi-kento* (L-shaped, at a corner, usually bottom right) and *hikitsuke-kento* (straight mark along the adjacent edge); the paper's corner sits in the L and its edge against the straight mark so each impression lands in the same place. — [Yoshabunko, Woodblock publishing (Wetherall)](https://www.yoshabunko.com/nishikie/articles/Woodblock_publishing.html); [JAANUS, kentou](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/k/kentou.htm); [MAU glossary, registration marks](https://art-design-glossary.musabi.ac.jp/registration-marks/)
- Full-colour *nishiki-e* used roughly ten to twenty blocks. — [JAANUS (Columbia)](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/3141)
- Full-colour prints appear from c. 1765, when registration was good enough to print several blocks accurately; earlier prints had one or two colours plus black. — [Harvard Arboretum, plant dye identification in Japanese woodblock prints](https://arboretum.harvard.edu/stories/plant-dye-identification-in-japanese-woodblock-prints/); [blog](https://hingstssignpost.blogspot.com/2017/02/the-evolution-of-color-in-japanese.html?m=1)
- Moisture changes in blocks/paper throw registration off; corrected by recutting kento. — [JAANUS, kentou](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/k/kentou.htm)
- Gauguin sometimes printed slightly off-register on purpose for a blurred, dreamlike effect. — [Grob Gallery](https://grobgallery.com/artworks/1441)

**Overprinting and transparency**
- Ukiyo-e pigments are transparent: a colour printed over the black line still reads black; blue over yellow gives green. — [Yoshabunko (Wetherall)](https://www.yoshabunko.com/nishikie/articles/Woodblock_publishing.html)
- *Kakeawase*: overprinting two colours to get a third, used to stay within block-count limits or cut costs, later valued for its own effect. — [JAANUS, kakeawase](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/k/kakeawase.htm)
- *Gomazuri* ("sesame printing"): described in JAANUS (as summarised) as a thin layer of one colour over another to suggest transparency, used e.g. for the rough texture of silk gauze robes. — [JAANUS, gomazuri](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/4354); [aisf mirror](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/g/gomazuri.htm)

**Bokashi (gradation)**
- *Ichimonji bokashi*: a straight horizontal band of gradation (like the character 一), used by Hokusai and Hiroshige for horizon, sea and sky; for wide areas it is repeated with different brushes, narrowing the shaded zone each time. *Ichimonji-mura bokashi*: same but with an uneven gradation edge, made by moving the brush irregularly. — [Wikipedia, Bokashi (printing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokashi_(printing)); [JAANUS, ichimonji bokashi](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/3857)
- *Atenashi bokashi* ("indeterminate gradation"): a kind of *fuki bokashi* where the area is wetted and pigment/water mixed on the block, giving soft, shapeless fades used for clouds etc. — [JAANUS, atenashi bokashi](https://aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/a/atenashibokashi.htm); [JAANUS, fuki bokashi](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/record/fukibokashi)
- Other forms: *kumadori bokashi* for small details (e.g., around eyes); *futa-iro bokashi* blends two colours toward each other by loading two inks on opposite edges of the brush; the wiping pattern follows the shape of the final shaded area. — [Wikipedia, Bokashi (printing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokashi_(printing)); [Barenforum, Basics of gradation printing (D. Bull)](https://barenforum.org/encyclopedia/entries/012_02/012_02.html)
- Bokashi, karazuri, kimedashi and furikake (sprinkled metallic/mica) are the main "special effects" adding depth and texture. — [FAMSF, Special effects in Japanese prints](https://www.famsf.org/stories/special-effects-japanese-prints)
- *Karazuri*: blind embossing from uninked carved blocks; *kimedashi*: larger raised areas pressed into paper (traditionally with the elbow, hence *niku-zuri*), printed last; the embossment can be pronounced. — [JAANUS, kimedashi](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/3083); [woodblock.com, Mystique #12 printing](https://woodblock.com/timeline/entries/2011/04/05_mystique_12_5_printing_finished.php); [Fitzwilliam, Yoshitoshi techniques](https://yoshitoshi.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/techniques/)

**Reduction ("suicide") prints**
- Picasso developed the reduction linocut c. 1958–1963 (possibly prompted by printer Hidalgo Arnéra): one block for all colours, cut further after each colour is printed, printing lightest to darkest; the block is destroyed, so the edition size is fixed. — [Minneapolis Institute of Art, Picasso cutting the bull](https://new.artsmia.org/stories/picasso-cutting-the-bull-in-printmaking); [Art Institute of Chicago, Still Life with Lunch I](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/195454/still-life-with-lunch-i); [Eames Fine Art, Picasso linocuts (PDF)](https://www.eamesfineart.com/usr/library/documents/main/artists/114/picasso-lino-cuts-.pdf)
- Mia holds successive states of *Le Banderillero* documenting the single-block reduction. — [VisualArtsDB, Mia Le Banderillero states](https://www.visualartsdb.com/artwork/mia-128217-le-banderillero-third-state)

**Other colour practices**
- Munch's jigsaw blocks (pieces inked separately, reassembled) leave white lines between colour zones. — [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/73795)
- Brücke woodcut flatness extended "then in colour too". — [Henze & Ketterer](https://henze-ketterer.ch/ausstellungen/expressive-woodcuts-brucke-style)
- TGP prints are mostly black only; later works sometimes add a second colour. — [MFA Boston, Vida y drama de México (TGP 1957)](https://mfaboston6.emuseum.com/objects/337869/vida-y-drama-de-mexico20-anos-de-vida-del-taller-de-grafica)

### Inferences
- Layer model: each colour block = binary (or bokashi-graded) mask × ink colour, composited with *multiply* (transparent water-based / transparent-base inks) so overlaps darken and mix (yellow × blue → green); the black key is printed last (Western) or first (ukiyo-e key block also printed early as proof) — in both cases colours over black stay black under multiply. Opaque oil inks (e.g., expressive linocut posters) need a partial opacity/"cover" parameter instead of pure multiply.
- Misregistration: one rigid offset per block (translation ~0.3–1.5 mm for careful prints, 1–4 mm for expressive/hand-registered prints; small rotation <0.5°), *not* per-pixel jitter; produces white paper slivers on one side of a shape and darker overlap on the opposite side. (Magnitudes are an inference; no measured values found.)
- Kento logic implies the error is consistent across the sheet (offset + slight rotation around the kento corner, i.e., bottom-right), possibly plus slight scaling from paper expansion when damp → offsets grow toward the far edges from the kento corner.
- Colour blocks are typically cut slightly smaller or larger than the key outlines ("trapping"); a realistic 2/3-colour mode should let colour shapes be simplified, softer and not exactly aligned with the key's edges.
- Bokashi presets: (1) ichimonji: linear gradient across a horizontal band at the top of skies/water, hard start edge, smooth fade, sometimes stepped by repeated passes; (2) ichimonji-mura: same with a ragged fade edge; (3) atenashi: soft, irregular, cloud-shaped fades. Gradients should show slight brush-stroke streaking parallel to the band.
- Reduction mode: colours from light to dark, each later layer a subset (smaller area) of the previous ones, all sharing one block's grain and one set of cut edges; dark layer overprints everything below.
- Embossing for Japanese mode: karazuri/kimedashi as a height map with no ink, rendered via subtle shading on the paper.

### Gaps
- No numerical data on typical misregistration magnitude in ukiyo-e or modern editions.
- No measurements of pigment transparency/hiding power for traditional pigments vs oil relief inks.
- The exact mechanism of gomazuri (speckled "sesame" texture vs transparent thin layer) is unclear from the summaries; the full JAANUS page could not be fetched.
- Printing order conventions (light-to-dark; key first vs last) were not explicitly sourced except for reduction.

## 6. Expressive vs refined traditions: distinguishing visual features

### Takeaway
Expressive traditions (Brücke, Nolde, Munch, Gauguin, TGP, contemporary posters) show the process: large flat black masses, angular/jagged gouge edges, visible tool marks and splinters, grain and knots, uneven hand inking/pressure, chatter and deliberate off-register. Refined traditions (Dürer, wood engraving, Vallotton, ukiyo-e) hide or discipline it: controlled swelling/tapering hatching (Dürer), perfectly flat, clean-edged blacks with white cut-out details and no hatching (Vallotton), and precise registration with transparent colours and gradations (ukiyo-e).

### Cited Findings
**Expressive**
- Brücke: woodcut prints the raised uncut surfaces, which suited their bold, flat style, also in colour. — [Henze & Ketterer](https://henze-ketterer.ch/ausstellungen/expressive-woodcuts-brucke-style)
- Kirchner exploited stark contrasts and the reductive possibilities of woodcut, heralding the "severely angular" style of Brücke's last years. — [MoMA, Kirchner, Dancer with Raised Skirt (1910)](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/64662)
- Kirchner and Brücke cultivated "bold, rough-hewn surfaces" and rawness for emotional directness; visible tool marks treated as part of the image. — search-summary attribution to [Made Good Designs, Kirchner: woodcuts, angles and raw line](https://madegooddesigns.com/?p=111164) (secondary design blog; low reliability) and MoMA Kirchner pages ([Nude Dancers](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/67181))
- Nolde brushed compositions onto the block with a broad brush and then carved; rough edges of forms keep the evidence of chisel and knife cuts; coarsely gouged areas, jagged lines and grain combine; portraits emerge from darkness with a few "splintered strikes". — [Karl & Faber lot](https://karlundfaber.de/it/aste/326/arte-moderna-day-sale/3260522); [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/articles/two-dancers); [MoMA, Prophet](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/62151); [MoMA, Head of a Woman III](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/69374)
- Munch: jigsaw-sawn colour blocks with white kerf lines, visible grain blocks. — [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/73795); [Nasjonalmuseet](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/en/collection/object/NG.K_H.A.19517)
- Gauguin: needle and sandpaper textures, hand printing with variable pressure/inking, deliberate off-register, no two impressions alike. — [NGA Australia](https://nga.gov.au/audio-learning-tours/gauguins-world/stop/302); [Grob Gallery](https://grobgallery.com/artworks/1441)
- Taller de Gráfica Popular: linocut became the preferred medium after WWII; black on cream paper; Méndez's carving described as intricate, interlaced yet dynamic, with sculptural tonal effects. — [AIC, Firing Squad](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/160038/firing-squad)
- TGP backgrounds split into simplified blocks of black and white. — [LACMA, Peones de la construcción](https://collections.lacma.org/node/207485)
- TGP: bold black-line prints on cheap linoleum, hand press, negative space cleared with V and U gouges; linocut can use large dark masses or engraved white lines. — [Atlas of Making, TGP political linocut](https://atlasofmaking.kenjiliu.com/techniques/tgp-linocut); [Britannica, linocut](https://britannica.com/print/article/342561)
- The Hammer Museum's "Gouge: The Modern Woodcut 1870 to Now" frames the gouge mark itself as the defining feature of modern woodcut. — [Hammer Museum (UCLA), Gouge exhibition](https://hammer.ucla.edu/exhibitions/2008/gouge-the-modern-woodcut-1870-to-now) (framing taken from the exhibition title; content not read)

**Refined**
- Vallotton: sharp contrast of large black areas and unmodulated white, outline and flat pattern emphasised, hatching-based modelling eliminated. — [Swann Galleries, Félix Vallotton](https://www.swanngalleries.com/profile/felix-vallotton/)
- Vallotton moved from linear black outlines toward solid black surfaces, with details appearing as cut-out white marks. — [Heidelberg University art archive](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/artdok/id/eprint/8245/contents)
- The Met credits Vallotton with reviving woodcut and links his flat blacks to ukiyo-e popular in Paris; *Intimités* (10 prints) is his best-known series. — [Met, Félix Vallotton: Painter of Disquiet](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2019/felix-vallotton-painter-disquiet/exhibition-galleries)
- Dürer: dense hatching plus swelling/tapering lines produce volume; much more supple lines than earlier heavy-outline woodcuts; so subtle that some scholars think he cut blocks himself. — [NGA](https://www.nga.gov/collection/art-object-page.141215.html); [Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/360117)
- Ukiyo-e: precise kento registration, 10–20 blocks, transparent overprints, bokashi, karazuri/kimedashi embossing. — [JAANUS](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/3141); [FAMSF](https://www.famsf.org/stories/special-effects-japanese-prints)

### Inferences
- Variant mapping for Trazo's Xilografía:
  - *Realista (refined)*: black-line or white-line hatching that follows form with swelling/tapering lines (Dürer) OR Vallotton-style flat black masses with white cut-out details; clean edges; faint grain; light, uniform speckle only in large solids; minimal chatter; register error ≤ ~0.5 mm.
  - *Expresivo*: large black masses, angular faceted cuts (straight-ish knife segments, not smooth curves), jagged/splintered contours, U-gouge clearing with visible scoop strokes and chatter in whites, strong grain and knots in blacks, baren mottle and salty speckle, occasional broken thin ridges.
  - *Dos tintas / tres tintas*: key block + 1–2 flat colour blocks composited by multiply, rigid per-block offsets (1–3 mm expressive, small for refined), colour shapes simplified and not matched to the key outline, optional Munch kerf lines or Gauguin off-register blur, optional reduction logic (light→dark subsets) and Japanese bokashi bands for skies.
- Cut direction rule: refined = cuts follow form contours/cross-contours with regular spacing; expressive = cuts follow gesture and grain, with spacing and width varying abruptly, and backgrounds simplified into blocky black/white areas (TGP).
- Cream paper + single black ink is the most recognisable TGP/poster cue.

### Gaps
- No technical study of Kirchner's or Méndez's cutting sequence or tool set was found.
- No quantitative comparison of line spacing/width between traditions; would require measuring scans (e.g., Met/AIC open-access images) — a recommended next step.
- Contemporary linocut poster conventions (e.g., overprinted opaque colours, "salty" speckle as style) are documented only in vendor/blog sources here.
